Question 2 — how a derived skill is told apart from a known one

Your own unit's skills come from the report and are certain. A foreign unit's come from a battle roster: they were true on the turn of that battle, which may not be this turn, and they are only the five skills a roster prints — a unit may have twenty others we will never see. A player must be able to tell the two apart at a glance.

One difference is already free. A report prints study points and a roster does not, so an own skill reads COMB 1 (30) and a derived one can only read COMB 5. That is a real signal, but a silent one — nobody learns it without being told. These options are about making it say something on its own.

And a second fact that shapes the choice. Levels really do move. Across one game's stored turns, 70 of 595 repeated observations disagreed with an earlier one, and 68 of those 70 were the level going up — units studying between battles. No unit ever lost a skill it had shown. So an old figure is a floor, not a lie, and the turn it came from is worth carrying.

The same four rows in every option: a foreign unit with one derived skill, one with three, one with nothing derived, and one of your own for contrast.

  • Brass already means “a figure we worked out” everywhere else in this table.
  • Colour alone is the weakest signal there is, and it is the one a player is most likely to read straight past.
  • Says nothing about when without a hover, and the hover is invisible until you go looking for it.

The cell names its source once, in the same italic dim the not disclosed line already uses, and then lists the skills plainly.

  • Unmissable, and it says why rather than leaving the reader to infer it from a colour.
  • Reads as the natural sibling of not disclosed — the same voice, the same place.
  • Spends 12 characters of a narrow column on every derived row, before a single skill is named.
  • Still silent about the turn, so it needs the hover anyway — and then M2 is doing more for less.
  • Sorting the column now sorts every derived unit together under “f”, which is either a feature or a nuisance and nobody has asked for it.
