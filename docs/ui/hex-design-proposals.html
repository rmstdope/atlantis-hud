Hex design proposals · five candidates

Five visual directions for a future map hex, drawn over the vocabulary in hex-rendering.md: terrain, settlement tiers, attitude-grouped units, guard, battle, monsters, lairs, shafts, gates, ships, buildings, roads, and stale knowledge. The neighbourhood is deliberately congested — most hexes stack many marks at once (city + battle + structures + guard + units; cave + shaft + battle + monsters + units) — because the real test of a hex design is not the sparse hex but the full one. Each design therefore states where every mark lives, so that any combination composes without collision. Every proposal is shown twice: flat terrain colours, and the application’s biome textures under the marks.

01Cartographer’s Table

A hand-drawn atlas. Muted pigment terrain, line-art glyphs and italic serif names. Every mark keeps a fixed compass anchor, like conventions on a survey sheet: guard banner NW, battle swords NE, name N, gate arch W, monsters E, workshops between them, shaft SW, lair and harbour SE, unit shields along the S edge. A full hex reads like a busy page of the same atlas, not a different design. Staleness is a parchment wash with pencil hatching; over textures, a light parchment gauze keeps the ink legible.

  • Warm and atmospheric; fits the play-by-mail fantasy register.
  • Every mark is a distinct pictogram — nothing depends on colour alone.
  • Fixed compass anchors mean the full city hex and the empty plain use one visual grammar.
  • At full occupancy the hex is dense; line-art needs the most zoom-band pruning of the five.
  • Muted palette leaves less contrast headroom for risk/route overlays.

02Tactical HUD

A modern strategy readout built for exactly this problem: seven reserved stations per hex — ship TL, battle TC, gate TR, shaft ML, monster MR, lair BL, building count BR — plus the settlement in the centre with its name along the S edge, unit counters above the name, and guard as an inner perimeter. A mark is present or its station is empty; nothing ever moves or collides. Stale hexes dim and state their age (“T-9”). Textures are dimmed hard so the readout stays a readout.

  • Congestion-proof by construction — the full mountain hex is as tidy as the empty tundra.
  • Numbers everywhere; counts and age are read, not estimated.
  • Station discipline makes scanning trainable: the eye learns where gates live.
  • Coldest of the five; the game’s fantasy flavour lives elsewhere.
  • All stations filled reads as a wall of badges; needs the zoom bands to thin it.

03Miniature World

A board-game diorama where a full hex is a full scene, composed like a painting with fixed grounds: the settlement holds the centre, the battle smokes over it, the guard stands at the NW approach, monsters prowl the eastern rim, the arch rises in the west, the pit sinks in the SW, cave and harbour share the SE shore, workshops sit NE, and the people gather along the bottom. Staleness is a grey wash, like fading memory. With textures on, the biome image replaces the painted decorations and the miniatures stand directly on it.

  • Most legible at a glance for new players — things look like what they are.
  • A congested hex reads as a rich scene rather than a pile of icons; density becomes flavour.
  • The style most flattered by the biome textures — diorama ground under diorama pieces.
  • Most drawing work per hex, and the scene composition rules are the most delicate to maintain.
  • Illustrations resist screen-constant scaling; needs per-band simplified forms.

04Emblem & Dots

Built on the premise that everything can be present at once: however much a hex holds, it shows one large medallion emblem — its most important fact by fixed priority (battle, then settlement, gate, shaft, lair, ship) — and every other feature drops into small coloured dots, up to two tidy rows along the bottom edge. Units are one proportional bar with a total — length is head-count, colour split is attitude. Guard is an inner perimeter; staleness dims the hex and dashes its rim. The congested city hex and the empty plain differ only in how many dots the row carries.

  • Degrades most gracefully under congestion — density never changes the layout, only the dot count.
  • A glance across the map reads the one thing that matters per hex; battles pop everywhere they happen.
  • The unit bar shows size and force balance in one mark.
  • The priority order is opinionated — a city in battle shows swords, and the city becomes a dot.
  • Dots need a legend; at speed, similar dots are hard to tell apart.

05Beveled Tile

A physical game board. Every hex is a raised tile — lit upper left, shadowed lower right, seamed to its neighbours — and everything on it racks into one chip grammar: the battle chip owns the top-centre, other feature chips fill the side rails (left first, then right), unit tokens with counts hold the bottom row, and the settlement is a central medallion whose size and pips give the tier. A full tile is a full rack, and a stale tile loses its bevel and sinks flush, dimmed and dash-rimmed.

  • Tiles separate cleanly at every zoom; the relief makes the lattice legible without heavy outlines.
  • One chip grammar for every feature — congestion fills rails predictably, and a future mark is one more chip.
  • “Sunken” staleness is instantly felt, before it is read.
  • Side rails hold five chips at most; beyond that the design has no answer but overflow.
  • Tile seams fragment continuous features — roads and routes visibly jump the gaps.

The same seven hexes in every proposal, deliberately congested so each design has to show its layout rules under load. Centre: city + battle + guard + three unit groups + seven buildings + a ship + roads. N: mountain with cave + shaft + battle + hostile guard + monsters + units + mines. NE: town + gate + guard + units + buildings. SE: sea battle with a fleet and two unit groups. S: lair + monsters + gate. SW: village + shaft + units. NW: a stale town sighting with buildings and a unit. Textures are the real biome images the application ships (config/public/biomes).
