Planning a voyage the ship is too heavy to make

The panel on the right of the map is where a player asks “how do I get this there?” — they press Plan move, pick a hex, and the panel answers with a route, a cost and a month, or with one red sentence saying why there is none. It already refuses a great many things. It does not refuse this one.

The situation everything below uses this one ship

A Longship sitting in open sea. The report states what it holds and what it can hold; the rules say a fleet cannot move at all while it is over capacity. The player asks for a one-hex hop south-east.

Question 1 — what stands where the route was pick one

The panel has one existing habit for a fleet that cannot go: a short crew already gets a red sentence and nothing else. The question is whether being too heavy is the same kind of “no”, or a “no, but here is what you’d get if you lightened it”.

OPTION A A flat refusal

Exactly what a short crew gets today: one red sentence, no route, no Apply button. Being too heavy is not a route problem — the ship isn’t going anywhere, so there is no route to show.

MovementPlan moveClear

This fleet is carrying more than it can hold: 210 aboard on a capacity of 150, so it will not sail.

  • Reads identically to the short-crew refusal already there.
  • No Apply button, so no order can be written by accident.
  • Doesn’t tell the player whether the trip was even worth unloading for.

OPTION B The refusal, and the voyage it blocks

The same red sentence, and beneath it the route the ship would have taken — dimmed, with no Apply button. A player deciding whether to spend a month shifting cargo can see what it buys.

Question 2 — the words pick one; all three are the same red line

The panel’s existing refusals are full sentences that name the numbers — “The crew cannot sail this fleet: it needs 4 levels of sailing, and the units aboard have 2.” These three differ in how much work they do for the player.

WORDS 1 Matches the crew line

This fleet is carrying more than it can hold: 210 aboard on a capacity of 150, so it will not sail.

  • Same skeleton as the crew refusal: problem, the two numbers, consequence.
  • The player does the subtraction.

WORDS 2 Names the number to fix

This fleet is 60 too heavy to sail: 210 aboard on a capacity of 150. Put 60 ashore and it will go.

  • The one number the player actually needs is the difference, and it is stated.
  • Turns a dead end into an instruction.
  • Longer, and the second sentence is a promise the panel can’t fully keep if the crew is short too.

WORDS 3 Short

Overloaded: 210 aboard, capacity 150.

  • Scans in one glance; fits a narrow panel on one line.
  • Doesn’t say it stops the voyage — the whole point.
  • Reads like a label, not like the panel’s other sentences.

Question 4 — too heavy and too few sailors pick one

Both can be true at once, and the panel shows one red sentence. The ship in our example with only 2 sailing levels aboard instead of 4 is over on both counts.

OPTION A One sentence, both reasons

This fleet will not sail: it is carrying 210 on a capacity of 150, and the units aboard have 2 levels of sailing where it needs 4.

  • The player fixes both before re-planning, instead of fixing one and being refused again.
  • A longer sentence, and a new shape alongside the two single-reason ones.

OPTION B Whichever is found first

This fleet is carrying more than it can hold: 210 aboard on a capacity of 150, so it will not sail.

…and after the cargo is shifted, on the next plan:

The crew cannot sail this fleet: it needs 4 levels of sailing, and the units aboard have 2.

  • Every refusal keeps exactly one shape. Nothing new to build.
  • The player unloads a ship, re-plans, and is refused again for a second reason nobody mentioned.
The rule: a fleet can move only if the total weight of everything aboard does not exceed the fleet’s capacity (rules/movement_sailing). A Longship carries 150 and needs 4 levels of sailing (data/Longship). Nothing here changes sailing speed, capacity, or any other route rule — only what the panel says before it offers you an order.
