Region decorations — what a province looks like on the map

Bead ah-6zv. Every hex carries a province name; nothing on the map yet shows where a province is. This page draws the same sample world three ways so the treatment can be chosen before anything is built. All three satisfy the bead: an outline around the union of the hexes known to belong to a province, and that province's name inside it.

Three separate questions are asked here — the treatment, what happens when a province's known hexes are in two pieces, and where the toggle lives.

Chosen. Treatment C, the atlas label — a soft dashed boundary and the name set widely letterspaced across the blob. Every connected piece is outlined and named, so a province found twice is written twice and no name ever floats over ground it does not own. The switch is an entry in the Badges menu called Regions, defaulting to on like every other badge.

And the label is fitted to the piece it names. A paper atlas sets a name to the size of the thing it names, so the label scales to the piece's width, clamped to a legible floor of about 42% of full size. A piece too small to hold its own name at that floor keeps the name anyway and lets it spill past the outline — which is what an atlas does with a small island, and what "name every piece" requires.

Question 1The treatment

Same data in all three: six provinces, one of them (Skeldar Reach, lower right) discovered as two separate blobs, and a scatter of hexes still unexplored.

AInk boundary

A thin brass line traces the union perimeter; the name sits at the centre in letterspaced small capitals with a dark halo. No fill at all — terrain, roads and badges read exactly as they do now.

  • Nothing is tinted, so terrain colour keeps its meaning
  • Cheapest to draw: one path plus one text per province
  • Boundaries between two adjacent provinces are a single shared line

BTinted march

The same boundary, plus a very faint wash of a per-province hue inside it, so neighbouring provinces are told apart at a glance without reading a name. The name sits in a small panel pill.

  • Extent is legible even where the map is dense
  • Adjacent provinces are obviously distinct
  • A second colour system on top of terrain and staleness

CAtlas labelchosen

A soft dashed boundary, and the name set large and widely letterspaced across the blob in the manner of a printed atlas — the province reads as a place rather than as a selection.

  • Most legible name; suits a map read at low zoom
  • Dashes say “approximately, as far as is known”
  • A big label crosses hexes and can hide unit badges

Question 2A province known in two pieces

Skeldar Reach has been found in two places with unexplored ground between them. The bead forbids one name floating in empty space between two unrelated blobs; these are the three ways out. Treatment A is used for the drawing.

1Name every piecechosen

Each connected blob is outlined and labelled independently. The name appears twice.

  • Never lies about extent; nothing is unlabelled
  • Repeats the name, and a one-hex sighting gets a full label

2Name the largest piece

Every blob is outlined, but only the biggest carries the name. Small outlying sightings are drawn silently.

  • One name per province, always
  • An outlined blob with no name is a small puzzle

3Name pieces above a threshold

Every blob is outlined; a blob of three hexes or more is named, a smaller one is not — so a stray sighting stays quiet but a real second front is named.

  • Middle course; scales as more of a province is found
  • A magic number the player cannot see or change
